Data Story · Singapore Maritime Energy

Singapore is not just moving more cargo. It is absorbing energy risk.

A 30-year port dataset shows Singapore shifting from volume growth toward larger vessels, bunker fuel services, and supply-chain resilience under Iran/Hormuz shocks.

The port story is about scale per ship.

Vessel calls rose slowly, while gross tonnage, container throughput, and bunker sales climbed much faster. Singapore's role becomes clearer when the port is treated as an energy and large-vessel service system.

Interpretation: the gap between vessel calls and gross tonnage is the project’s first resume-ready insight: Singapore is serving larger ships and more energy-intensive shipping activity, not simply more arrivals.

The 2026 Hormuz crisis is a different class of shock.

The two periods have different observed Brent price paths. This is a descriptive event-window comparison, not a causal estimate.

Iran is the switch, not the supplier.

Direct imports from Iran are essentially gone after 2019. The exposure is indirect: Gulf-origin oil and refined products depend on a chokepoint whose risk is shaped by Iran.

The long-run trend shows diversification away from Gulf dominance. But diversification does not remove chokepoint risk; it changes how the risk is redistributed through suppliers.

Saudi/UAE made up a larger share of reported oil-import value in 2026Q1.

When the shock hit, Singapore did not simply abandon the Gulf. The share from Saudi/UAE rose, while Americas and Asian refinery-trade shares fell.

The new story for a resume.

The Q1 source shift was already apparent before February 28: Gulf import-value shares were 37.7% in January and 45.0% in February, then 35.5% in March. Causality is untested.
